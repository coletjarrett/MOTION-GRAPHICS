This is a new way to make broadcast graphics:
lower thirds, titles, timelines, maps, and short animated films.
Nothing here was keyframed by hand.
Every graphic is a small program.
[pause 0.4]
The program answers one simple question:
what does the picture look like at this moment in time?
Because each frame depends only on time,
any frame can be drawn on any computer, in any order.
So an ordinary laptop can render many frames at once,
and the result is identical, every time.

Here is the workflow.
It begins with a brief: the words, the style, and the length.
An AI assistant writes the template, renders test frames, and checks its own work.
A second AI model reviews the result, and reports defects with timestamps.
And a person stays in charge of every decision:
the look, the wording, and the final cut.

Rendering happens in a standard web browser, running invisibly, one frame at a time.
The frames are encoded for editors:
ProRes with transparency for overlays, and H.264 for review.

Because the text is data, one template becomes hundreds of clips.
Change a name, a date, or a language, and render again in seconds.
Right-to-left scripts, like Arabic, mirror automatically.

And because the templates are code, they can be versioned, reviewed, and shared between teams.
The same method scales from a simple name title
to a fully narrated, animated film.
Modest in cost. Consistent in quality.
And ready to serve in many languages.
